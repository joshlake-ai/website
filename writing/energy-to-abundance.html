Energy → Tokens → Intelligence → Invention → Abundance

Eleven observations from a few months of building with agents, plus a lot of podcasts, videos, and Substacks.

The short version. Data centers use energy and GPUs to make tokens. Models use tokens to generate intelligence. Humans, and increasingly autonomous agents, use that intelligence to invent new things. New things make abundance. That sequence is how I think about the world, AI, and the future.

These are my observations and learnings. Almost none of it is original to me. Here are the eleven.

Intelligence unlocks invention. Invention unlocks abundance.

1. Intelligence is a stack of multipliers.

We are going to have 1,000x and then 1,000,000x intelligence capacity. How? Stack compounding gains.

Model efficiency. Falling token cost. Parallel agents. Better memory and context. Better algorithms. Intelligence here means useful capacity to do work, and every one of those five raises it.

Each one is worth some multiple on its own. Stack all five and 1,000x is the conservative read. Stack them again and a million becomes a word you can say out loud without embarrassing yourself.

Everybody watches one line on one benchmark and misses that these compound. They are also largely independent. Progress on any one of them does not wait on the other four. Price is headed to zero at the same time.

So here is the challenge. What is possible with 1,000x intelligence capacity? With 1,000,000x? Be careful with that one. It will blow your mind.

2. The impacts of invention are unfathomable.

Invention will be 1,000x automation. First chat. Then code. Now automation and agents.

Automation makes what we already have cheaper. Invention makes things exist that did not exist before. Both matter. The second one is much bigger.

Most of the new stuff will be combinatorial, which is exactly what these models are already good at. Drugs. Materials. Recipes. Movies. Products. Take the pieces that already exist, search the space of ways to put them together, and test the results faster than any human team could.

The question I keep coming back to is whether you can automate the invention process itself. Materials, projects, products. Run the loop, generate candidates, test them, keep what works, and go again. I believe the answer is increasingly yes.

Mark Zuckerberg argues a version of this in The Future Is For Everyone. His frame is that personal superintelligence should go to individuals rather than getting concentrated in a few institutions, and that the point of the technology is to help people create and achieve more of what they want. I read it as the same claim from the other direction. The payoff is what gets made that would not otherwise exist.

3. Agents are the great catalyst.

This is the workhorse today. Loops, graphs, and a goal. We got promised automation for thirty years and kept getting workflow tools. Agents actually do the work.

I run Claude Code every day and I spent last weekend inside Grok Bot. OpenClaw and Hermes Agent are pushing on the same thing from different directions. There will be online stores of agents for every task, or you will piece them together yourself with LLMs, MCPs, and APIs. It starts to look a lot like manufacturing engineering. You spend less time writing software and more time developing the workflow.

Efficiency and productivity are necessary and they are great. That is how you drive costs down. Driving costs down is what frees everybody up to go invent.

4. The product overhang from today’s models is enormous.

LLMs are good enough. Go look at Artificial Analysis. Hundreds of models across dozens of labs. Gemini, Claude, ChatGPT, Qwen, xAI, and the whole run of Chinese models. Open and closed. Small and cheap, large and expensive.

All of them highly capable already. We are just getting started on what sits on top of them. Tools, agents, harnesses.

If every lab stopped training tomorrow we would still have a decade of products to go build. The gap between what the models can already do and what anyone has shipped is the largest opportunity on the board right now. Do it now. Do not wait.

5. We are going to build 1,000x more software.

One user. Perfectly customized. Used once and thrown away. Run locally, spun up instantly, never reviewed, never shared.

Minimal bells and whistles. Not meant for production. Not meant to persist. All the complexity and the calcs abstracted away so the person using it never sees them.

It may not even look like software. Excel is software with a UI so good that the coding is abstracted away, and the formulas sitting on top of the database are very real. Everyone is already a builder. More and more stuff is just code.

Software exists to solve a problem. When the marginal cost goes to zero you build the solution and then you delete it.

6. Outcomes > Output. Pay for performance is back.

People will pay for performance. Results matter more than effort. The underlying software stops mattering.

Inside companies this shows up as speed to innovate beating cost savings. That is a different budget and a different buyer. Do not buy software. Buy results.

7. Vibe-living.

This is the ultimate luxury and the natural extension of outcomes over output.

You request an outcome and you name the spend. I will put in $X for Y result. Then things get done, built, designed, and fixed.

Underneath, that is an auto-combo. Software, robots, and capital assembled on the fly against a verifiable outcome, then taken apart once the outcome is met. A temporary assembly that existed to produce one result.

Vibe coding was the small version of this. The big version is that you stop specifying how any of it happens. Verification is what makes it work. Define done, prove done, and you can put money against it and let the system work out the rest.

A world of abundance abstracts away a lot of the effort and inputs it takes to get to a result. Ozempic for the physical world.

8. Agent routers and model routers will be huge.

There are too many models, agents, and tools to keep up with. Router companies, or the frontier labs themselves, will do it for us.

Every request gets routed on capability, task complexity, cost, quality and reliability, data privacy, security, scalability, and repeatability. General model or industry-specific model. Cheap and fast for the easy 80 percent, expensive and slow for the rest. That saves a lot of money.

Nobody is going to hand-pick a model per task for long. We are already seeing tool and agent proliferation resolve the same way Zapier recipes did, into prepackaged workflows that solve one specific thing. Whoever owns that layer owns a lot.

9. Voice finally works.

Voice frickin’ works. Huzzah.

It is fast, it is accurate, and the old bugs are gone. No more fighting the transcript or cleaning up every third word.

I talked most of this piece into Claude Code. That matters more than it sounds like it does. The input method was the bottleneck on how much thinking actually made it out of my head, and that bottleneck is gone.

10. There has never been a better time to start a company.

The cost of turning an idea into a real thing is falling off a cliff. One person with a stack of agents covers work that used to take a floor of people. Design, build, test, ship, support.

Everything above compounds into this. Cheap intelligence, an enormous product overhang, disposable software, buyers who want outcomes, and routing that keeps the bill down. The barrier to starting has never been this low, and the ceiling on what a small team can reach has never been this high.

More companies, smaller and stranger and faster. More things that exist because one person could finally make them.

Output is nearly free now. Outcomes are the only thing left worth managing.

11. Speed to power is the limiter.

Energy is what is limiting the acceleration of this whole sequence.

Every link in that chain runs on electrons. Energy and electrification are the long-form bottlenecks to abundance, and it is not close. Abundance requires very low prices on raw inputs, and the rawest input is power. High price electricity is the single biggest blocker to all of it.

So cheap electricity is the only path forward. Solar and storage, nuclear, geothermal, wind, SMRs, ten different storage ideas, balcony solar, all of it, and far more of it than anyone is currently planning for. Building more is also how you prove the economics and how you get prices down, and getting prices down is what massively accelerates the sequence.

The grid has more buffer in it than people assume, which means the near-term answer is less about steel in the ground than about flexibility, orchestration, and getting out of our own way. Solar and batteries. Behind-the-meter gas. Repurposing existing commercial and industrial facilities that already have interconnection. Modular data centers. Data centers overseas. Data centers in space. Anything except waiting in line for interconnection.

Energized megawatts are the binding constraint on the next decade, ahead of chips, capital, and talent. That is the argument I made in Not All Tokens Are Equal.

Most people aren’t ambitious enough about any of this. An abundant future does not arrive on its own. It needs to get built.

The future is for everyone. Mostly it is for the people who start building now.
