Invention > Automation

Eight observations from a few months of building with agents.

The short version. Energy makes tokens. Tokens make intelligence. Intelligence unlocks invention, and invention unlocks abundance. Automation makes what we already have cheaper, which is fine and small. Invention makes things exist that did not exist, which is enormous. Agents are how the invention loop gets run, and skills are how it compounds. Energy and electrification are the long-form bottlenecks, so cheap electricity is the only path forward. The jobs story is an abundance story. Taste becomes the scarce skill, and we do more and better for each person. Most people aren’t ambitious enough. An abundant future needs to get built.

I have spent the last few months building with agents nearly every day. Not evaluating them, not writing about them from the outside. Building with them. Building stuff in the real world is fun, and building it with other people is better.

Here is the chain I keep coming back to. Energy makes tokens. Tokens make intelligence. Intelligence unlocks invention. Invention unlocks abundance. Each link is a real constraint, not a metaphor, and the whole chain only moves as fast as its tightest link.

Which is another way of saying the cost of anything creative is heading toward zero. Writing, art, code, design, music, recipes, legal work, materials, chemistry, teaching, simulation, optimization. All of it. Here is what I think happens on the way.

Intelligence unlocks invention. Invention unlocks abundance.

1. Invention beats automation.

Mark Zuckerberg published The Future Is For Everyone earlier this month. The core of it: invention, not automation, will be the greatest contribution of superintelligence. He argues for putting a personal superintelligence in everyone’s hands instead of concentrating it in a few labs, predicts more companies rather than bigger ones, and expects an abundance of jobs rather than a shortage of them. You can hold whatever view you want about Meta and still see that the framing is right.

Automation makes the thing you already have cheaper. Invention makes things exist that did not exist before. The second number is much larger than the first.

Most of what gets invented from here will be combinations. New drugs and new chemistry. New movies. New recipes and new food. New content. New products, and new materials to make them out of. Combinatorial search is exactly the kind of work that gets cheap first.

Which raises the question I keep circling: can you automate the invention process itself? Not the write-up, the actual loop. Hypothesize, build, test, learn, repeat, with agents running most of the loop and a human holding the taste. If that works even partially, everything downstream of it changes.

2. We are going to build 1,000x more software.

Not 20 percent better software. Three orders of magnitude more of it. Software for every problem and every workflow, and almost none of it looking like what we currently call software.

The new default piece of software has one user. It is perfectly customized to that person. It gets used once and thrown away. It runs locally. Nobody reviews the code. It spins up instantly. It never gets shared. No bells and whistles, no production hardening, no roadmap. All the complexity and the calculations are abstracted away behind it. It is transient by design and not meant to persist.

Almost all software went unwritten because writing it cost more than having it was worth. That entire space just opened up. Most of what fills it will be small and ugly and used by exactly one person, exactly once. That is the point, not a problem.

The same thing happens to content. If a lesson can be generated on the fly for one learner, you get a 1:1 tutor for any subject, taught live, at the level that person is actually at. We have known for decades that one on one tutoring beats everything else we do. It was never affordable. Now it is.

3. Agents mean automation, finally.

Chat gives you advice. Agents do the work. A chatbot tells you how to reconcile the invoices. An agent reconciles them at 2am and leaves you the three it could not figure out.

Agents can be as narrow or as wide-ranging as you want. They just need to do work that is economically valuable to someone. That is the whole bar.

Which is why the way to start is not to build “a customer service agent.” Build the thing that solves one specific, annoying, named problem. Reviewing invoices. Commissioning docs. Incentive forms and rebate paperwork. Proposals and SOW creation. Electrical design. Technical escalation. Recruiting screens. Training videos. My own list from Elephant Energy runs a few dozen deep, and every line on it is a real agent somebody should build.

What comes next is stores. Marketplaces of agents, one for damn near every task, wired together with an LLM in the middle and MCPs and APIs as the connectors. That is not programming. That is manufacturing engineering. You are designing a line: which station does what, in what order, to what tolerance, and where the rework loop goes.

My rule: if you are going to do it twice, write a skill. If you are going to do it five times, you should have written the skill last month.

4. The product overhang from today’s models is enormous.

If frontier training stopped tomorrow, we would still have a decade of products to build out of what already exists. The capability is in the box. What is missing is everything around it: context, memory, permissions, interface, trust, and the workflow it plugs into. Most companies are pointing frontier intelligence at email subject lines. That is a race car doing laps in a parking lot.

5. Outcomes beat output.

Agents make output nearly free, which means output is now worthless as a measure of anything. Lines of code, tickets closed, decks produced, words written, all noise. Results will matter more than effort, and the underlying software stops mattering too. Nobody is going to ask which stack delivered the result.

What do people actually pay for? Decisions, speed, and outcomes. They are going to pay for performance, not for hours or seats.

The same correction applies inside companies. Speed to innovate beats cost savings, and it is not close. Almost every AI rollout I see is aimed at the smaller of the two prizes.

Output is nearly free now. Outcomes are the only thing left worth managing.

6. Agent routers and model routers will be huge.

Model routers are the obvious half. Cheap model for the easy task, expensive model for the hard one, and at any real scale that decision is worth serious money. Duh.

Agent routers are the bigger half, and nobody has nailed it yet. Once you are running forty agents and a hundred skills, the routing call has to weigh capability, task complexity, cost, quality and reliability, data privacy, security, scalability, and repeatability. Soon it also has to choose between a general model and an industry-specific one, because HVAC and automotive and marketing and legal are all going to get their own. That is not a prompt. That is a control system.

It is also a dispatch problem, and dispatch is familiar ground. Match work to capacity with incomplete information while the clock runs. I spent years on exactly that at Nuro and at Elephant Energy. Whoever builds the good version of it owns the layer above everything else.

7. We will sell well-trained skills for robots.

What made agents genuinely useful in software was skills. Write the procedure once, reuse it forever, compose it with everything else. Robots get the same thing next, and they get a market for it. Start with the dull, the dangerous, and the dirty.

Fold the laundry. Unpack the groceries. Mow the yard. Water the plants. Pick up the toys. Unload the dishwasher. Chop the vegetables. Each of those is a discrete, well-trained skill that somebody will sell, and each one is small enough to be real in the near term.

I spent five years watching how long it takes to train a good field technician, and how much of a company’s value lives in the heads of the best ones. When that training becomes a file you can copy, the constraint on the physical economy changes completely.

8. Vibe-living.

Vibe coding was the preview. The general version is that you request an outcome and it happens. You say what you want done, built, designed, or fixed. You say what you are willing to spend to get it. Things get done.

Underneath, that is an auto-combo: software, robots, and capital assembled on the fly against a verifiable outcome, then torn down when the outcome is met. Not a company, not an app. A temporary assembly that existed to produce one result.

Push that far enough and you get idea-to-object, where the physical world starts to mirror the digital world’s speed. Verification is what makes it work. If you can define done and prove done, you can put money against it and let the system work out the rest.

The bottleneck is energy

Every link in that chain runs on electrons. Energy and electrification are the long-form bottlenecks to abundance, and it is not close. Abundance requires very low prices on raw inputs, and the rawest input is power. High price electricity is the single biggest blocker to all of it.

So cheap electricity is the only path forward. Solar and storage, nuclear, geothermal, wind, SMRs, ten different storage ideas, balcony solar, all of it, and far more of it than anyone is currently planning for. Building more is also how you prove the economics. And the grid has more buffer in it than people assume, which means the near-term answer is less about steel in the ground than about flexibility, orchestration, and getting out of our own way.

The binding constraint on the next decade is not chips or capital or talent. It is energized megawatts, which is the argument I made in Not All Tokens Are Equal.

What happens to work

The honest part first. There will be real displacement, and it starts where the work is most repeatable and least physical. Customer support is the obvious one. A lot of outsourced knowledge work sits in the same bucket. Saying otherwise is not optimism, it is avoidance.

But the shape of what comes after is genuinely good, and I do not think most people have worked it through.

When intelligence goes to zero, the bottleneck moves. Selection, direction, taste, and deployment become the scarce things. Trust becomes the currency. The valuable human roles move upstream and downstream at the same time: deciding what should exist, and then making it real, trusted, and adopted. Taste turns out to be the distinguishing skill, which is a strange and hopeful thing to write down.

Then there is what I have been calling labor as an experience. If human capacity is freed up and coordination gets cheap, you do not do less. You do more, better, for each person. Five people on one haircut because it is faster and nicer. The best doctor in the world giving you a full hour. Classrooms of four. Service that is faster, better, and more of it. The experience becomes the product.

That is the switch from an efficiency mindset to an abundance mindset, and it is the whole ballgame.

The doom version of this story is that agents do the work and then there is no work. I do not buy it. Every time the cost of making something collapsed, we did not make the same amount of stuff with fewer people. We made vastly more stuff, and invented jobs nobody could have described in advance.

The cost of turning an idea into a real thing is falling off a cliff right now. That is not a threat to work. It is the largest expansion of who gets to build anything that I have seen in my lifetime. More companies, smaller and stranger and faster. More things that exist because one person could finally make them.

Most people aren’t ambitious enough about what that means. An abundant future does not arrive on its own. It needs to get built.

The future is for everyone. Mostly it is for the people who start building now.
